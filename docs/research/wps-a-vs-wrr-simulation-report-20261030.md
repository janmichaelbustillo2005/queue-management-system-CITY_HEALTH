# WPS, WPS-A and WRR under two queue structures — simulation report

Date: 2026-10-30 · Code: `integration/d1-wpsa-wrr` @ `6213aa0` (latest verified integration) plus the report
tooling on branch `docs/sim-wpsa-wrr-report` · Results: [`simulation-results-20261030/`](simulation-results-20261030/)
· Method: [`scheduling-simulation-methodology.md`](scheduling-simulation-methodology.md)

> **Synthetic data only.** Every patient, arrival time, consultation length and category share in this report
> was generated by the simulator from documented assumptions (`backend/simulation/scenarios.js`). No number
> here was measured at the Cabadbaran City Health Office. The hosted database was not accessed, and the live
> queue, its scheduling settings and the adviser-demo system were not changed. Read every result as "under
> these assumptions, the model behaves like this", not as a measurement of the clinic.

## 1. What was run

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Simulator entry point | `backend/simulation/run.js` (no npm script; run with `node`) |
| Report tooling (new) | `backend/simulation/report.js`: tables, paired effects, CSV, SVG charts, sample trace. It only reads `results.json`; for the trace it reruns the unchanged engine on one seed with an observer around each policy. |
| Tests | `backend/test/simulation.test.js` (7) and `backend/test/simulation-report.test.js` (5, new): 12/12 pass |
| Policies | WPS (original), WPS-A (10-minute aging, +2 cap), WRR (weights = priority score) |
| Queue structures | **service** (legacy: consultation/check-up → Doctor 1, prenatal/maternity → Doctor 2, family planning → Doctor 3; three queues) and **shared** (D1 Model A, provisional: three general doctors take the head of one queue) |
| Scenarios | low-volume, medium-volume, high-volume, priority-surge, unequal-workload, long-waits (all 6 documented) |
| Replications | 30 paired seeds per scenario: **20261029 – 20261058** |
| Simulated days | 6 scenarios × 30 seeds × 3 policies × 2 structures = **1,080** |
| Runtime | Node.js v24.21.0, about 2 s on the development PC |

**Paired design (common random numbers).** For each seed the simulator generates one day of patients: arrival
times, services, priority categories and consultation lengths. All six runs (3 policies × 2 structures) serve
exactly those patients, with the same three doctors available all day (no breaks), and a patient's consultation
lasts the same time whichever doctor or policy serves them. The only things that change are **who waits in which
queue** (structure) and **which waiting patient is called next** (policy). A per-seed difference therefore
isolates one factor, and its 95% confidence interval is much narrower than comparing two independent averages.

## 2. Separating queue structure from scheduling policy

The headline question has two parts, and the paired design answers them separately
([`paired-effects.md`](simulation-results-20261030/paired-effects.md)):

![Structure versus policy effect](simulation-results-20261030/charts/effects-structure-vs-policy.svg)

**Queue structure (shared − service, both under WPS), maximum wait, minutes:**

| Scenario | Average wait | P95 wait | Maximum wait | Seeds improved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| low-volume | −4.7 ± 1.1 | −20.6 ± 5.9 | −30.4 ± 7.5 | 30 of 30 |
| medium-volume | −22.1 ± 6.1 | −78.0 ± 21.4 | −96.4 ± 19.7 | 30 of 30 |
| high-volume | −46.9 ± 6.7 | −167.7 ± 24.4 | −183.0 ± 22.0 | 30 of 30 |
| priority-surge | −19.7 ± 5.3 | −102.1 ± 31.4 | −146.3 ± 33.8 | 30 of 30 |
| unequal-workload | −100.2 ± 14.5 | −268.4 ± 30.1 | −276.9 ± 28.5 | 30 of 30 |
| long-waits | −48.0 ± 6.6 | −182.0 ± 22.4 | −194.5 ± 19.5 | 30 of 30 |

**Scheduling policy inside the shared queue (versus WPS), maximum wait, minutes:**

| Scenario | WPS-A − WPS | WRR − WPS |
|---|---|---|
| low-volume | 0.0 ± 0.0 | −0.3 ± 0.3 |
| medium-volume | −0.1 ± 0.2 | −1.0 ± 0.6 |
| high-volume | −0.8 ± 1.0 | −1.1 ± 1.1 |
| priority-surge | 0.1 ± 0.3 | −4.0 ± 1.8 |
| unequal-workload | −0.2 ± 0.3 | −0.4 ± 0.8 |
| long-waits | −0.7 ± 0.8 | −1.1 ± 1.3 |

Inside the legacy service dispatch the policy effect is larger only where one doctor is overloaded by a burst:
in priority-surge WRR shortens the maximum wait by 38.2 ± 10.8 min and the P95 by 24.8 ± 8.5 min (28 of 30 seeds);
WPS-A changes the maximum wait by at most 0.5 min in any scenario. Average waits move by less than 0.3 min under
any policy change, in either structure.

![Policy effect alone](simulation-results-20261030/charts/effects-policy-only.svg)

**Reading:** in this model, how patients are distributed to doctors matters one to two orders of magnitude more
than which waiting patient is called first. Sharing one queue among three general doctors shortened the
maximum wait on every one of the 180 paired days; changing the scheduling rule changed it by minutes.

## 5. Throughput and doctor utilization

![Throughput](simulation-results-20261030/charts/throughput.svg)
![Utilization](simulation-results-20261030/charts/utilization.svg)

Throughput is patients served ÷ hours until the last consultation ends; the same patients are served in every
run, so a higher value means the clinic finishes the same work sooner. Policy changes throughput by less than
0.05 patients/h and utilization by at most 0.01; the structure changes both:

| Scenario | Throughput service → shared (patients/h) | Utilization D1 / D2 / D3, service | Utilization D1 / D2 / D3, shared |
|---|---|---|---|
| low-volume | 7.88 → 8.00 | 0.59 / 0.33 / 0.35 | 0.60 / 0.42 / 0.28 |
| medium-volume | 12.05 → 13.45 | 0.92 / 0.51 / 0.50 | 0.80 / 0.73 / 0.63 |
| high-volume | 12.84 → 17.08 | 0.98 / 0.54 / 0.55 | 0.94 / 0.92 / 0.90 |
| priority-surge | 11.65 → 12.60 | 0.88 / 0.49 / 0.50 | 0.77 / 0.68 / 0.57 |
| unequal-workload | 9.46 → 14.27 | 0.99 / 0.26 / 0.20 | 0.80 / 0.74 / 0.64 |
| long-waits | 12.92 → 17.86 | 0.99 / 0.54 / 0.55 | 0.97 / 0.96 / 0.95 |

Under service dispatch Doctor 1 (consultation and check-up, about 55% of the assumed demand) is the bottleneck
while Doctors 2 and 3 are idle half the day; the shared queue spreads the same work across all three. In the
shared model the lower-numbered doctor is preferred when several are idle, which is why D1 > D2 > D3 at light load.

## 6. Protected-priority ordering

![Protected ordering](simulation-results-20261030/charts/protected-ordering.svg)

Two counts per simulated day (all decisions are logged; values: mean ± 95% CI):

* **Lower score served while a higher score waited.** Zero for WPS by construction. WPS-A does this on purpose
  when an aged patient overtakes (up to 7.7 ± 1.6 per day, long-waits shared). WRR does it whenever a class's turn
  comes (up to 11.4 ± 1.7 per day).
* **Regular served while a Senior, PWD or Pregnant patient waited.** **Zero for both WPS and WPS-A in every
  scenario and structure**, because a Regular's effective score is at most 3, which never beats a Senior (4) and
  loses ties to PWD/Pregnant (3) on base score. **WRR does it 0.2–6.2 times per day** (shared) and 0.4–5.7 (service).

## 7. WRR versus WPS-A: head to head

The two candidate policies are compared directly, day by day, on the same synthetic patients
([`head-to-head.md`](simulation-results-20261030/head-to-head.md), [`.json`](simulation-results-20261030/head-to-head.json)).
"Effective" is defined first, as a two-part decision rule, so the comparison does not depend on which number is
picked afterwards:

1. **Priority-lane compliance (must pass).** On no simulated day may a Regular patient be called while a Senior,
   PWD or pregnant patient is waiting. This mirrors the priority-lane principle the system enforces.
2. **Then** prefer the compliant policy with the shorter P95 and maximum waits, especially for Regular patients.

| Dispatch | WPS | WPS-A | WRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Service (legacy) | 180 of 180 days compliant | 180 of 180 | **31 of 180** |
| Shared queue (D1) | 180 of 180 | 180 of 180 | **36 of 180** |

![Head to head](simulation-results-20261030/charts/head-to-head-wrr-vs-wpsa.svg)

WRR − WPS-A, mean ± 95% CI over 30 paired days (days on which WRR was lower / higher):

| Scenario / dispatch | Maximum wait (min) | Longest Regular wait (min) | Longest Senior/PWD/Pregnant wait (min) | Regular before waiting protected (/day) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| priority-surge / service | −38.2 ± 10.8 (28/0) | −37.7 ± 11.0 (28/0) | −6.9 ± 11.7 (14/14) | +5.2 ± 0.8 (0/30) |
| priority-surge / shared | −4.1 ± 1.8 (21/3) | −4.6 ± 2.1 (21/3) | +1.5 ± 1.8 (8/17) | +2.8 ± 0.6 (0/29) |
| high-volume / service | −5.8 ± 6.8 (3/1) | −6.3 ± 7.3 (4/1) | +14.6 ± 5.2 (1/27) | +4.7 ± 0.8 (0/29) |
| long-waits / service | −4.5 ± 5.8 (3/0) | −5.0 ± 6.6 (3/0) | +15.1 ± 8.4 (2/26) | +5.7 ± 0.8 (0/30) |
| long-waits / shared | −0.5 ± 1.2 (13/17) | −0.6 ± 1.2 (15/15) | +4.0 ± 1.7 (5/24) | +6.2 ± 0.8 (0/30) |

Average wait and throughput do not separate the two (both are work-conserving; average-wait differences are
at most 0.2 min). **Reading:** WRR shortens the worst waits of Regular patients, clearly only in the surge
scenario under service dispatch, and it does so by calling Regular patients ahead of waiting protected patients
on most days, which also lengthens the longest protected waits in most scenarios. WRR therefore fails rule 1 in
both structures, and **WPS-A is the more suitable of the two under this rule**; WPS-A and the original WPS are
nearly identical (Section 2), so the remaining question between them is the aging policy itself (Section 10).
A different rule, for example one that accepts a few protected-lane violations in exchange for shorter worst
cases, could change this conclusion and would need the Health Office's agreement.

The key moment, **09:55:04, Doctor 1 becomes free, 15 patients waiting:**

| Policy | Ranking it sees (top) | Calls |
|---|---|---|
| WPS | P053 Indigenous **2** (arrived 13 s ago) > P032 Regular 1 (waited 55.7 min) > … | **P053 Indigenous** |

## 9. Discussion for the defense and Chapter 4

Suggested wording (adapt as needed):

> A discrete-event simulation with 30 paired synthetic days per scenario was used to compare the original
> Weighted-Priority Scheduling (WPS), WPS with 10-minute aging (WPS-A, +2 cap) and Weighted Round Robin (WRR)
> under two queue structures: the legacy fixed service-to-doctor dispatch and a shared queue served by three
> general doctors. All policies and structures served identical simulated patients. In every scenario, sharing
> the queue reduced the maximum waiting time on all 30 paired days (by 30 to 277 minutes on average depending on
> load), whereas changing the scheduling rule changed the maximum wait by at most a few minutes in the shared
> queue. WPS and WPS-A never called a Regular patient while a Senior, PWD or pregnant patient was waiting; WRR
> did so up to about six times per simulated day, and roughly doubled the longest Senior waiting times in the
> busiest scenarios. WPS-A mainly re-ordered patients with adjacent scores; its most visible side effect was longer
> waits for Indigenous patients, who can be overtaken by Regular patients after 20 minutes. Because the
> workload is synthetic, these results show the expected direction and relative size of each effect, not the
> waiting times that City Health clients actually experience.

Points to stress when asked:

1. **Synthetic versus real.** The model's parameters are assumptions; the results establish mechanisms
   (structure dominates, WRR trades protected-category priority for shorter worst-case waits) that should be
   confirmed with the clinic's own arrival counts, service times and category shares.
2. **Why WPS-A barely matters here.** The +2 cap means a Regular can rise to 3 at most. Under overload the
   longest-waiting Regulars are already at the cap, so aging no longer re-orders them; only the shared queue
   (more doctors serving the backlog) shortens their wait.
3. **Why WRR is not recommended as the live scheduler.** It improves the worst case only by serving lower
   classes ahead of waiting protected patients, which conflicts with the priority-lane principle the system is
   designed to enforce.

## 10. Limitations

**WPS-A's +2 cap**

* The cap bounds how far aging can lift a patient (Regular 1 → 3, Indigenous 2 → 4, PWD/Pregnant 3 → 5), so it
  cannot bound the maximum wait under sustained overload: once the queue's Regulars all sit at 3, they are again
  ordered only by arrival. Long-waits maxima are unchanged (322 → 322 min service, 127.5 → 126.9 shared).
* It reshuffles protected categories among themselves: a PWD or pregnant patient who has waited 20 minutes
  (3 → 5) is called before a newly arrived Senior (4), and an Indigenous patient waiting 20 minutes ties a new
  Senior (Senior wins by base score). These are policy decisions for the adviser and Health Office (I2 policy
  risk 5).
* It raises Indigenous waits, because aged Regulars and Solo Parents (1 → 3) overtake a new Indigenous patient (2).
* Constants (10-minute step, +2 cap) were proposed, not derived from clinic data; the model did not search for
  better values.

**WRR's priority-ordering trade-offs**

* WRR serves classes in turns, so lower classes are served while higher-priority patients wait (0.2–6.2 Regular
  calls per day ahead of waiting Senior/PWD/Pregnant patients in the shared queue).
* Senior waits roughly double in surge and overload scenarios; the gain is concentrated on Regular and
  Indigenous maximum waits.
* Weights equal the priority score and classes are distinct scores (combined categories form their own classes);
  other weightings were not explored.
* WRR is a comparison baseline only; it is not implemented in the live queue.

**Simulation model**

* Synthetic workloads; parameters in `scenarios.js` are assumptions.
* Three doctors available all day, identical speed per service, no breaks, no registration or call delays,
  no no-shows, recalls or patients leaving.
* The shared structure follows D1 Model A, which is provisional and pending approval; the service structure
  follows the legacy fixed mapping.
* Throughput is defined relative to the time the last patient finishes, not the 8-hour window.
